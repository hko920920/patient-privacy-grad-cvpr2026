## Summary

This paper proposes PP-Mark, a provenance framework for images generated by diffusion models that combines a lightweight, latent-space watermark with a publicly verifiable zero-knowledge proof (ZKP) based on SP1. The key idea is to bind the watermark's statistical detection rule to cryptographic commitments tied to the generation context (prompt, seed, model) and the image hash, so an image is accepted only if it both passes a correlation-based detection threshold and carries a valid proof attesting to protocol compliance. The authors provide a formal unforgeability claim under standard assumptions and empirically evaluate PP-Mark against representative post-hoc and semantic watermarking baselines, showing strong resistance to black-box imprint transfer and white-box optimization attacks while maintaining practical verification and proving overheads for decentralized auditing.

## Strengths

### Technical novelty and innovation
- The paper introduces a principled fusion of semantic watermarking and zero-knowledge proofs, moving verification from pure statistical inference to cryptographic attestation.
- The context-bound binding (prompt, seed, model) and Merkle-committed latent trace, combined with a ZKP that references the image hash, represent a novel, thoughtfully integrated design that specifically addresses the "public detector ⇒ optimization oracle" vulnerability.
- Uses a realistic zkVM (SP1), with engineering details and end-to-end timing, demonstrating feasibility rather than a purely conceptual proposal.

### Experimental rigor and validation
- Evaluates multiple attack surfaces: black-box imprint transfer (direct and cross-method), strong white-box (PGD) forgery with differentiable pipelines, removal (regeneration, steganalysis), and common transformations.
- Includes both SD 2.1 and SDXL; thresholds calibrated at FPR=1%; reports runtime, score concentration vs sample count N, and perceptual quality trends (BRISQUE p95).
- Clear distinction between two operating modes (score vs accept) with threat/operational profiles and empirical evidence that the ZKP prevents forged acceptance even when score can be adversarially manipulated.

### Clarity of presentation
- The overall motivation and system flow are clear: embedding, commitment, and proof generation on the producer side; inversion, score computation, and proof verification on the verifier side.
- The optimization vulnerability are well articulated.

### Significance of contributions
- Addresses a critical and timely problem: publicly verifiable provenance for generative media in the presence of strong forgeries that exploit public detectors.
- The cryptographic anchoring concept is broadly relevant to community efforts (watermarking, attribution, and content provenance) and aligns with emerging standards/regulatory needs.

## Weaknesses

### Technical limitations or concerns
- The zk-circuit constraints are under-specified. The paper explicitly states the circuit "does not resimulate the full latent embedding or inverse-CDF" and enforces only "minimal consistency," but it is unclear whether the circuit actually checks the computational relations gi = F⁻¹(H(b‖i+1)), si from bits(b), and zi = gi + α·si for the opened subset. Without explicitly enforcing these relations (even only for the opened elements), the proof risks certifying only binding/commitment consistency rather than that the prescribed embedding was executed.
- Accept-mode proof binds to the exact image hash (SHA-256 of pixels), which precludes any post-processing and sharply limits practical applicability of the cryptographic acceptance in the wild (where recompression/resizing is common). The paper notes score-mode as a fallback, but then the core claim "prevents adversarial optimization against the detector" no longer holds strictly (score-mode PGD FAR=0.76 on SD2.1).
- The unforgeability theorem (Theorem 3.5) reduces to zk soundness and hash collision resistance but does not explicitly account for the partial opening (k_open ≪ N) nor specify which embedding relations are verified inside the circuit. The current proof sketch feels too high level for a security claim central to the paper's contribution.

### Experimental/methodological issues
- Sample sizes are relatively small in several tests (n=100 per condition), with limited diversity (prompt-only dataset); more comprehensive datasets and ablations (e.g., across content types, photorealism/illustrations, different schedulers) would strengthen claims.
- Some baseline comparisons mix heterogeneous detectors/objectives (e.g., converting binary APIs into "bit-accuracy" scores), which may introduce calibration artifacts; a careful discussion on comparability limits and potential advantage to PP-Mark's score should be expanded.
- No reporting of proof sizes/bandwidth overheads. For decentralized verification at scale, artifact size and distribution are critical.

### Clarity or presentation issues
- Ambiguity between "signing key" and the "secret key k" used to derive b. The text uses "forged binding" scenarios where the adversary "controls the signing key" but not k; the roles and key hierarchy should be made explicit to avoid confusion (e.g., is there a separate metadata signing key distinct from the binding key k?).
- The circuit specification (Definition 3.1–3.2 and "Circuit Constraints") lacks the exact checks required to certify execution of the embedding rule. This is central to the claimed "compliance proof."

### Missing related work or comparisons
- Limited positioning relative to cryptographic provenance frameworks such as C2PA-style signed manifests, canonicalization, and robustness trade-offs (beyond a brief contextual mention).
- The emerging literature on instance-adaptive or two-sided detection (e.g., ISTS) that target forgery/removal trade-offs could be discussed in more depth to better position PP-Mark's complementary guarantees and limitations.

## Detailed Comments

### Technical soundness evaluation
The high-level design is sound and addresses a real gap: public, decentralized verification without turning the public detector into an attack oracle. Binding the detector to a cryptographic proof that references the image hash is a strong idea.

However, the zk-circuit's exact constraint set is the linchpin. If the circuit fails to enforce that the opened elements' gi and zi are computed from b via the specified F⁻¹ and watermark equation, the proof certifies little beyond that "some trace was committed." Even with partial openings, it should at minimum check the embedding relation for opened indices to support the "compliance" claim. As written, this is ambiguous and weakens the formal assurance.

The formal theorem focuses on standard soundness/collision-resistance. It would benefit from a precise security game capturing the partial-opening audit and the exact relations checked. Otherwise, the theorem might not fully capture attack surfaces where the witness is maliciously crafted to match commitments without reflecting a valid embed.

### Experimental evaluation assessment
The attack suite is broad and relevant (imprint transfer, white-box optimizations, regeneration, steganalysis, common transforms) and includes both SD2.1 and SDXL. The separation between score- and accept-mode is borne out empirically; accept-mode stays at FAR=0 by construction.

Score-mode's white-box vulnerability (FAR 0.76 on SD2.1; 0.11 on SDXL) is clearly presented; this is an honest and important characterization: cryptographic acceptance is the security-critical layer.

Runtime numbers and proving times are useful; still missing are proof sizes (bytes), witness sizes, and network/bandwidth implications for public distribution, which matter for practical deployment.

### Comparison with related work (using the summaries provided)
- Relative to the survey (2510.02384), PP-Mark addresses a key challenge identified broadly in watermarking: robust, verifiable provenance under adversarial conditions, and moves beyond detector-only strategies.
- Compared to multiplicative fingerprint removal (2508.03067), which undermines attribution via universal attacks, PP-Mark's accept-mode is designed to be unforgeable without the secret key, thereby invalidating forged or altered content regardless of optimization. The experiments emphasize exactly this distinction between detector-only schemes and crypto-bound acceptance.
- ISTS strengthens instance specificity and detection to resist removal/forgery; PP-Mark makes a complementary contribution: cryptographic non-repudiation and unforgeability, at the cost of exact-image binding in accept-mode. A more explicit comparison to instance-adaptive strategies and two-sided detection could clarify how PP-Mark's statistical layer (score-mode) compares in forgery/removal trade-offs, and how the ZK layer complements rather than replaces such defenses.

### Discussion of broader impact and significance
The proposal meaningfully advances the state of publicly verifiable provenance in a space where private detectors and closed APIs have been the norm. It aligns with regulatory and industry needs for auditable, decentralized verification.

The strict pixel-hash binding in accept-mode is both a feature (prevents replay on modified content) and a limitation (does not tolerate benign transforms common in the wild). The paper's candid separation of "accept" vs "score" operational modes is appropriate; exploring canonicalization/robust hashing or proof-of-derivation for benign transforms is an important future direction.

## Questions for Authors

1. Precisely which relations are enforced inside the zk-circuit? Do you verify for each opened index that gi = F⁻¹(H(b‖i+1)), si derived from bits(b), and zi = gi + α·si (or the normalized variant), or do you only verify Merkle consistency of arbitrary trace entries? Without checking the embedding equation for the opened set, how does the proof certify protocol compliance rather than just consistency of a commitment?

2. What are the proof and witness sizes (in bytes) for typical settings (e.g., N=1000, k_open=32)? How do these scale with N, and what are the bandwidth/storage implications for decentralized distribution at scale?

3. Can you clarify the key hierarchy? Is the "secret key k" used to derive b the same as the "signing key" used in Sig-only/Sig+Score baselines? In Table 3's "forged binding" attack, the adversary "controls the signing key" but not k—please explain the realistic scenario and how PP-Mark(score) prevents this without verifying a ZK proof.

4. How sensitive are results to the inversion method/hyperparameters (e.g., steps, scheduler, VAE versions)? Did you evaluate alternative inversion settings or schedulers (e.g., DDIM vs DPM-Solver) and how do thresholds transfer?

5. Accept-mode binds to SHA-256 of raw pixels. Have you considered canonicalization strategies (or robust hash commitments plus a proof of benign transform) to make accept-mode usable under common distribution-channel transforms (resizing, JPEG re-encoding)?

6. What happens if the binding key k is compromised? Is there a key rotation/revocation story that allows downstream verifiers to distrust old receipts without global breakage?

7. For fairness of baselines with binary outputs (TrustMark, InvisMark), could you share sensitivity analyses that quantify how "bit-accuracy" as a continuous proxy may advantage/disadvantage them relative to PP-Mark's continuous score?

## Overall Assessment

This paper tackles an important and timely problem—publicly verifiable provenance for AI-generated images—by anchoring a latent-space watermark to a zero-knowledge proof of compliance. The design insight that public detectors become optimization oracles is well-motivated, and binding verification to cryptographic evidence is a compelling response. The experimental section is broad and reinforces the main message: score-only modes remain vulnerable to optimization, while cryptographic acceptance eliminates forgery in practice (and by design). The work is original and of clear interest to the NeurIPS community.

However, the paper's central security claim relies critically on what the zk-circuit actually proves. As written, the constraint set appears under-specified; if the opened elements are not checked against the embedding relations driven by b (including the F⁻¹ mapping and watermark equation), the ZK layer risks certifying only metadata consistency, not that embedding was executed. Theorem 3.5 is correspondingly high-level and does not explicitly capture partial-openings or the precise relations enforced. Additionally, accept-mode's pixel-exact binding limits real-world usability under common image transforms; while the authors acknowledge this and propose score-mode for robustness, the trade-off should be discussed more deeply, and practical mitigations (canonicalization/robust commitments) should be explored.

Overall, I find the contribution significant and the idea strong. **I would lean toward acceptance** if the authors clarify and strengthen the circuit specification (even if only for the opened subset), provide proof/witness sizes, and cleanly disentangle the key roles and the "forged binding" scenario. With these clarifications, the paper would make a solid, impactful contribution.
